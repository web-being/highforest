What a terpene is

Plants build terpenes from five‑carbon pieces called isoprene units.

  1. 5 carbons

    One unit

    Isoprene, C5H8.

  2. 10 carbons

    Two units make a monoterpene

    Light enough to leave a leaf at the touch of a finger. Drawn: myrcene, one of its two units in colour.

  3. 15 carbons

    Three units make a sesquiterpene

    Heavier and slower to evaporate. Drawn: humulene, its first and third units in colour.

Add an oxygen atom and chemists call the result a terpenoid. Outside chemistry all of them are called terpenes, and these pages follow that habit.1

Plants make them to repel what eats the leaf and to attract what pollinates the flower, and they are part of how a plant responds to heat and injury.2 The smell of a forest is largely terpenes in the air.

Same atoms, six smells

Six of the eleven share one formula, C10H16: ten carbons and sixteen hydrogens joined six different ways. They smell different because they are shaped differently.

  • Limonenebright, citrus, fresh, lightly sweetOne six-membered ring with a tail. The molecule exists in two mirror-image forms; citrus peel makes almost only this one.
  • α-Pinenefresh, coniferous, resinous, herbaceousTwo fused rings, one of them a strained four-carbon square. The double bond sits inside the ring.
  • β-Pinenegreen, woody, resinous, pine-likeThe same atoms and the same two fused rings as α-pinene, with the double bond outside the ring instead of inside it.
  • Myrceneearthy, herbal, balsamic, hop-like, gently fruityIt has no ring. Its ten carbons form an open, branched chain with three double bonds: two isoprene units joined head to tail.
  • α-Phellandrenefresh, green, citrus-like, lightly pepperyA six-membered ring carrying two double bonds side by side.
  • Terpinolenefresh, herbal, softly pine-like, lightly floralLimonene’s close relative: the same ring, with the tail’s double bond turned toward it.

Common questions

Why add terpenes to honey?
For the aroma. Citrus and conifer make a morning honey, floral and berry notes an afternoon one, earthy and hop-like notes an evening one. You meet them when the jar is opened, when the honey is tasted, and when it is stirred into a drink.
Is this a cannabis product?
No. Our honeys contain no cannabis, THC or CBD, and nothing intoxicating. Terpenes are not particular to cannabis: lemon peel, lavender, pine needles, hops and black pepper are full of them.1 2
Are terpenes safe to eat?
People have always eaten them in fruit, herbs and spices.1 2 As food flavourings, ten of the eleven have been evaluated by the FAO/WHO committee JECFA, with no safety concern at current levels of intake for eight.26 Honey itself is not for infants under one year.24
Where do the terpenes come from?
We buy them food-grade from True Terpenes, whose facilities are certified to ISO 9001:2015 and FSSC 22000. Each batch is tested for terpene content, solvents, pesticides and heavy metals, and comes with a certificate of analysis that we review.
Does hot water drive the aroma off?
Some of it. None of these compounds boils in a cup, but all of them evaporate from it, faster the hotter the water. We suggest about 50 °C. Boiling points.
