An atlas of botanical terpenes

Terpenes are the molecules that make a lemon smell of lemon and a pine wood of pine. This atlas describes the eleven used in High Forest honeys: what each one is, where it grows, how it smells, and what research has and has not shown.

Read this first

In these honeys terpenes are flavourings. High Forest has not published how much a jar contains, and nobody has tested the honeys themselves. Most of the research cited here was done in animals. In the human studies, doses ran from milligrams inhaled to grams swallowed. This atlas describes the ingredients and makes no statement about what a jar of honey does. How to read the research

What a terpene is

Plants build terpenes from five-carbon pieces called isoprene units. Two units make a monoterpene: ten carbons, light enough to leave a leaf at the touch of a finger. Three units make a sesquiterpene: fifteen carbons, heavier and slower to evaporate. Add an oxygen atom and chemists call the result a terpenoid. Outside chemistry all of them are called terpenes, and this atlas follows that habit.1

Plants make them to repel what eats the leaf and to attract what pollinates the flower, and they are part of how a plant responds to heat and injury.2 The smell of a forest is largely terpenes in the air.

    Limonene

    C10H16 Monoterpene

    After the lemon, Citrus limon. One six-membered ring with a tail. The molecule exists in two mirror-image forms; citrus peel makes almost only this one.

    Scent
    bright, citrus, fresh, lightly sweet Reference description: “citrus odor” (HSDB)
    Found in
    lemon, orange, grapefruit and lime peel, juniper berries, peppermint, rosemary, fennel, dill, caraway and celery seed.

    2633

    PubChem 440917
    Systematic name
    (4R)-1-methyl-4-prop-1-en-2-ylcyclohexene D-limonene, the (R)-(+) form of citrus peel

    What has been studied highest rung: people, small

    • PeoplePeople absorb it. A 1998 dose-finding study gave patients with advanced cancer 0.5 to 12 g per square metre of body surface a day by mouth and followed it through the blood.9
    • PeopleIn a 2013 pilot study, women with early-stage breast cancer took 2 g a day for two to six weeks; limonene collected in breast tissue.10
    • PeopleIn a 2024 laboratory study of 20 adults, vaporized limonene on its own (1 or 5 mg) could not be told from placebo. Inhaled at 15 mg together with 30 mg of THC, it reduced the anxiety THC caused.11
    • Not knownWhether the small amounts used to flavour food do anything beyond flavour. The studies in which people swallowed it used grams.

    α-Pinene

    C10H16 Monoterpene

    After the pine, Pinus. It is the main component of turpentine. Two fused rings, one of them a strained four-carbon square. The double bond sits inside the ring.

    Found in
    pine, spruce and fir, rosemary, juniper, sage, eucalyptus, basil, dill and parsley, frankincense.
    In High Forest

    Molar mass
    136.23 g/mol
    Boiling point
    155–156 °C JECFA, HSDB
    CAS number
    80-56-8
    FEMA number
    2902
    Record
    PubChem 6654
    Systematic name
    2,6,6-trimethylbicyclo[3.1.1]hept-2-ene

    What has been studied highest rung: animals

    • AnimalsIn a 2017 study, mice given 10 mg per kg did better on learning and memory tests after their memory had been impaired with the drug scopolamine.7
    • ReviewA 2019 review summarizes cell and animal work on inflammation, microbes and nerve cells, and notes how few studies there are.6
    • Not knownAny effect on memory or attention in people. No human trial of the isolated compound is cited here.

    Myrcene

    C10H16 Monoterpene

    After Myrcia, a genus of the myrtle family. It has no ring. Its ten carbons form an open, branched chain with three double bonds: two isoprene units joined head to tail.

    Scent
    earthy, herbal, balsamic, hop-like, gently fruity Reference description: “pleasant; terpene odor” (HSDB)
    Found in
    hops, mango, lemongrass, thyme, bay laurel, basil, parsley, cardamom.
    In High Forest
    Soothing Berry Grounding Rest as a flavouring ingredient; amount not published
    Molar mass
    136.23 g/mol
    Boiling point
    166–167 °C JECFA
    CAS number
    123-35-3
    FEMA number
    2762
    Record
    PubChem 31253
    Systematic name
    7-methyl-3-methylideneocta-1,6-diene

    What has been studied highest rung: animals

    • AnimalsIts sleepy reputation comes from rodents given large doses. A 2021 review found animal results for pain, inflammation and sedation, and no human studies.15
    • SafetyIn a two-year study, male rats given very large amounts by stomach tube developed kidney tumours, and male mice liver tumours (US National Toxicology Program, 2010). The International Agency for Research on Cancer classed myrcene in 2019 as possibly carcinogenic to humans, group 2B.20 25
    • SafetyIn 2018 the US FDA removed synthetic myrcene from its list of flavourings. The law requires this for any additive that causes cancer in animals at any dose; the FDA stated that it poses no risk to public health at the amounts used in food. Myrcene occurs naturally in hops, mango and thyme.21
    • Not knownWhether it sedates people at all. No human trial exists.

    C15H24 Sesquiterpene

    After the clove tree, once called Caryophyllus aromaticus. The old name is printed on the plate. A nine-membered ring fused to a four-membered one, a rare arrangement in nature.

    Scent
    peppery, woody, warm, gently spicy
    Found in
    clove, black pepper, hops, basil, oregano, rosemary, lavender, cinnamon leaf, copaiba.
    In High Forest
    Sunshine Soothing Berry Grounding Rest as a flavouring ingredient; amount not published
    Molar mass
    204.35 g/mol
    Boiling point
    256 °C JECFA
    CAS number
    87-44-5
    FEMA number
    2252
    Record
    PubChem 5281515
    Systematic name

    What has been studied highest rung: animals
