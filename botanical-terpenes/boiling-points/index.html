Terpene boiling points

Boiling points of eleven plant terpenes, each with the source that reported it. Ten are at atmospheric pressure; humulene is listed only at reduced pressure.

The eleven Boiling points Do terpenes do anything?

In the cup

None of these molecules boils in a cup of warm water; they evaporate from it. Boiling point is a rough guide to how readily: the lower it is, the more easily a compound evaporates. How fast each one leaves a cup also depends on how well it dissolves, and that has not been measured for these honeys.

High Forest suggests water at about 50 °C, not boiling. Hotter water drives aroma off faster, so more of it is gone before you drink.

Check the pressure

A liquid boils at a lower temperature under reduced pressure, and some reference values are measured that way. A boiling point quoted without its pressure can be far off.

PubChem carries two figures for β-caryophyllene: 256 °C at atmospheric pressure, from JECFA, and 264 to 266 °F, about 130 °C, at 14 mm Hg, from CAMEO Chemicals. Read as an atmospheric value, the second would make one of the two heaviest compounds here the most volatile of all. If a chart gives caryophyllene a boiling point near 130 °C, this is its likely origin: the two match to the degree.

Humulene is missing from the chart for the same reason. PubChem lists its boiling point only at 3 mm Hg.
