Do terpenes do anything?

They give plants and food their smell. Whether they do more in a person is mostly untested. Ten of the eleven terpenes here have been studied only in cells and animals, limonene has been measured in people, and none has been through a randomized trial as a treatment.

Reading the research

Most research on terpenes stops at cells and animals. Each entry in the atlas marks how far it has reached for that compound, on four rungs.

  1. CellsActivity seen in cells or isolated tissue. Shows that something can happen, not that it does in a body.All eleven have passed this rung.
  2. AnimalsAn effect measured in rodents, often at large doses.α-Pinene, β-Pinene, Myrcene, α-Phellandrene, Terpinolene, Linalool, Geraniol, Citronellol, β-Caryophyllene, Humulene
  3. People, smallMeasured in people: how it is absorbed, or a small laboratory study.Limonene
  4. People, trialsRandomized, placebo-controlled trials of the compound itself as a treatment.None. Lavender oil, which contains linalool, has trials of its own.

How much is a study dose?

A heaping teaspoon of honey weighs about 8.5 g. Choose how much of it is terpenes, and compare the result with the doses given to people in the studies cited here. The scale is logarithmic: each step to the right is ten times more.

By the nose, more likely than by the mouth

If scented honey changes how someone feels, smell is the plausible route. In mice, linalool’s calming effect vanished when the sense of smell was removed.13 In people the measured effects are small. A controlled trial of aromas found that lemon scent lifted reported mood and left stress hormones, heart rate and blood pressure unchanged.22 A meta-analysis of lavender found signs that inhaling it eases anxiety, in studies too mixed in quality to settle the question.23 Nobody can be blinded to a smell, so expectation is hard to rule out. Scented honey itself has not been tested.

Four mistakes

“Terpenes are a cannabis thing.”
Cannabis is one of many plants that make them. Lemon peel, lavender, pine needles, hops, black pepper and rose petals are full of them, and people have always eaten and breathed them.1 2
“Each terpene has its effect: one for energy, one for sleep.”
That idea comes from studies of single compounds given to rodents at high doses. In the one controlled human test cited here, inhaled limonene on its own could not be told from placebo.11 For myrcene, the “sleep terpene”, no human study exists.15
“If it worked in a study, it works in the cup.”
Most findings come from animals given large doses, or from people who swallowed between 80 milligrams and several grams a day. How a flavouring amount compares depends on how much terpene the honey holds, and High Forest has not published that. See the scale above.
“Natural means harmless at any dose.”
Myrcene, natural in hops and mango, caused tumours in male rats and mice given very large amounts for two years.20 The amounts used to flavour food are far smaller, and the US Food and Drug Administration judged them no risk to public health.21 Honey itself is not for infants under one year.24
